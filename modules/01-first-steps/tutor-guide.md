# Week 1 tutor guide

**Session:** Your first steps in amateur radio · **Duration:** 90 minutes · **Group:** beginners

Use the [learner guide](learner-guide.md) as the explanatory text. These notes support a discussion-led class, not a verbatim lecture.

## Prepare the room

- Open or print the [current EX307 booklet](https://rsgb.org/main/clubs-training/forms/), the worksheet and quiz. Keep the answer key for the review.
- Have a whiteboard, a metre rule or tape measure and a projector if available.
- Display the two original SVG diagrams. They also work as ordinary printed figures.
- An optional receiver/SDR can make the subject tangible. The lesson does not depend on live reception or internet access once the resources are downloaded.
- If showing a transceiver, prevent accidental transmission during this introductory session. Use receive-only equipment or a suitable transmit-inhibit arrangement. Any separate on-air demonstration must be prepared and directly supervised under the applicable licence conditions.

Explain that learners can ask for a question to be rephrased. Introduce one acronym at a time, let people work in pairs, and give them time to read numbers before answering aloud. Do not rely on colour to identify bands or waves.

## Run the session

| Minutes | Segment | What the tutor does | Evidence of learning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0–8 | Start with a purpose | Ask what a radio could do on campus. Introduce frequency, band and mode using the display example. | Learners offer a use and identify a question they need answered. |
| 8–18 | Amateur radio and licensing | Discuss hobby versus business use; introduce the three levels and the exam/licence distinction. | Pairs explain worksheet Activity A. |
| 18–32 | A shared spectrum | Teach the 3–30–300–3,000 sequence. Sort the frequency cards in Activity B. | Each learner classifies one example and explains why tuning capability is not permission. |
| 32–48 | Waves and units | Compare the two wave plots; measure a metre; practise the official chart with Activity C. | Learners state both units and predict what happens when frequency doubles. |
| 48–53 | Short break | Pause and invite quiet questions. | — |
| 53–63 | Modes | Explain the mode table; run Activity D as pair matching. | Learners distinguish a mode change from a frequency change. |
| 63–73 | Read the reference table | Work through Activity E, including the whole 144–146 MHz row. | Learners find status and power, and notice units and qualifications. |
| 73–85 | Individual quiz | Allow about 8 minutes to answer and 4 to compare selected reasoning. Permit EX307 for Questions 8 and 12. | Collect uncertain topics as well as scores. |
| 85–90 | Exit check and homework | Ask the three exit questions below; explain the 30-minute practice task. | Each learner identifies one idea they can explain and one to revisit. |

If reading the reference booklet takes longer, finish reviewing the quiz as homework. Preserve the discussion of misunderstandings instead of rushing into extra terminology.

## Explanations that usually help

**Why a licence?** A transmitter can affect people who are nowhere near the room. Permission and operating skills help us share the resource responsibly. Keep geographical restrictions, call-sign details and licence administration for Week 2.

**Frequency versus wavelength:** draw the same pattern with one horizontal axis labelled time and a separate horizontal axis labelled distance. Ask what each axis lets us measure. In the frequency illustration, count complete cycles, not just the number of visible peaks.

**Approximate band names:** use 145 MHz ≈ 2.07 m and 14.2 MHz ≈ 21.1 m. Explain that the names “2 metres” and “20 metres” are conventions. If someone calculates 20 m as 15 MHz, their physics calculation is right; 15 MHz is not thereby an amateur allocation.

**Chart reading:** model one frequency-to-wavelength example, then one in the reverse direction. Explain logarithmic spacing. Accept estimates appropriate to the printed graph, rather than demanding calculator precision.

**Mode vocabulary:** AM, FM and SSB concern ways of modulating a signal. Data modes and SSTV describe wider ways of carrying messages or pictures. An introductory list does not make them mutually exclusive technical categories. Students need recognition now; waveform detail returns in Week 4.

**Sharing:** primary does not mean sole owner. Secondary operation must protect primary services. Do not imply that an apparently silent frequency is automatically available to transmit on.

## Scope and assessment

The [source map](sources.md) identifies partial coverage. In particular, this week does not complete licence conditions, AC theory, waveform recognition or operating procedure.

The quiz is formative. As a RARS teaching target, aim for **10/12 with an explanation of corrected mistakes**. This is not an official pass mark and does not predict the Foundation exam result. Revisit Questions 1, 10 and 11 even when the total score is high: the reasoning matters for responsible use.

Use the answer key to choose a focused follow-up. A learner who remembers “2 metres” but cannot explain the relationship needs a different activity from a learner who understands the idea but misreads MHz as kHz.

## Exit questions

1. What changes when we change the frequency, and what changes when we change the mode?
2. If frequency increases, what happens to wavelength?
3. Where would you check whether a transmission is permitted?

## Homework: about 30 minutes

- 10 minutes: reread the sections connected to any quiz mistakes.
- 10 minutes: repeat the chart and table lookups without following the worked answers.
- 5 minutes: make three flashcards for the HF/VHF/UHF boundaries.
- 5 minutes: prepare a one-minute explanation of a radio activity you would like to try.

Optional listening is enrichment, not a requirement. If you add it, arrange an amateur-band receive-only demonstration and have a fallback explanation when no signal is present.
