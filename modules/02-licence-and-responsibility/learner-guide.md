# Week 2 learner guide

## 1. A licence is permission with conditions

Last week we asked what a radio can do. This week we ask what you may do with it.

Imagine RARS has a handheld radio on the table. A new member wants to try it, another suggests a frequency, and a third plans to take it abroad. Each question involves permission, not just the equipment's capabilities.

**RSGB administers the examination; Ofcom issues the licence.** Passing the exam does not itself authorise unsupervised transmitting. Apply for the licence through the official process, then operate within its conditions. The personal licence levels are Foundation, Intermediate and Full. [S1, S3]

Keep your licence and its associated conditions available. The personal document, conditions booklet and applicable Notice of Coordination work together; a screenshot of a frequency table is not the whole licence. [S3]

### Keep the record up to date

Notify Ofcom immediately in writing if your name or recorded address changes. Confirm that your licence details remain valid at least once every five years. This is revalidation: a long-lasting licence is not something you can obtain and then forget. Breaching the conditions can lead to revocation. [S1, S2]

**Campus example:** moving accommodation may change your recorded address. Do not wait until the next five-year check to report an actual change. Follow Ofcom's current update process and retain confirmation.

## 2. Your call sign identifies the station

A call sign is the identity assigned through licensing. It is not a nickname you invent for a contact. For example, `M7ABC` illustrates a Foundation-style call sign; this example must not be used on air unless it is genuinely assigned to the station being operated.

### Regional secondary locators

A **regional secondary locator**, or **RSL**, is inserted as the second character to indicate the UK nation or Crown Dependency where the station is operating. It describes the station's location, not the operator's nationality or home address.

| Station location | RSL for an individual |
| --- | --- |
| England | E |
| Guernsey | U |
| Isle of Man | D |
| Jersey | J |
| Northern Ireland | I |
| Scotland | M |
| Wales | W |

An RSL is optional for an ordinary Foundation call sign, but it must be correct if used. The important exception is an Intermediate call sign beginning with **2**, which must include an RSL. [S1, S2]

Using our classroom example, `M7ABC` can become `MW7ABC` when an RSL is used in Wales, or `MM7ABC` in Scotland. An operator who stays in Scotland should not use W merely because they normally live in Wales.

### Optional suffixes

A suffix follows a slash, such as `/P` for portable operation. It is optional, must not obscure identification and must not be offensive. An RSL and a suffix do different jobs: one indicates the region, the other can give additional operating information. Neither extends the licence's permissions. [S1, S3]

### When should you identify?

The station must be clearly identifiable at all times. Transmit its call sign as frequently as practicable, using voice or a suitable format for the mode. Do not learn an invented universal rule such as "identification is only needed every 15 minutes". [S2]

For Foundation exam preparation, practise identifying when there is a change of **frequency, mode or digital protocol, supervisor, or RSL if one is being used**. Those changes are specifically highlighted in the syllabus. Begin and finish your classroom contact practice with clear identification as good operating practice. [S1]

## 3. Letting someone else try the radio

Someone without a licence can operate with permission under the licensee's **direct physical supervision**. The supervisor must be able to take back control, ensure the conditions are followed and remains responsible. Being available by phone or "somewhere on campus" is not the same arrangement. [S2, S3]

| Situation | Identity and conditions to use |
| --- | --- |
| You operate under your own valid licence | Your own call sign and your own licence conditions |
| A learner operates under a personal licensee's direct supervision | The supervisor's call sign and licence conditions |
| A licensed visitor operates independently under their own licence, with equipment permission | The visitor's call sign and licence conditions; borrowing a radio does not upgrade the licence |

**Example:** a Foundation licensee borrows a Full licensee's radio but operates independently with their own call sign. They still have Foundation privileges. A more capable transmitter is not a more capable licence.

If a supervisor leaves and the learner has no independent authority to transmit, stop transmitting. For this week's exercise, use a disconnected microphone or ordinary conversation rather than a live station.

A society name alone is not a club licence. A **Full (Club)** licence has separate operator and supervision provisions involving an authorised Full licensee. Do not assume every member may use a club call sign independently. [S2]

## 4. Messages: who is the communication for?

The amateur service supports learning, technical investigation and leisure. Using it to dispatch paid work or advertise a commercial service does not fit that purpose.

In ordinary operation, communicate with other amateur stations on permitted amateur frequencies. An initial **CQ** call invites another amateur station to answer. An established **net** is a group conversation involving at least three amateurs, with communication and identification established; it is not a licence to run a public broadcasting station. [S1]

**Discuss:** why is "CQ, is another amateur station available for a contact?" different from announcing a shop's special offers to everyone within range?

The licence includes specific arrangements for emergencies, User Services, beacons and data stations. These are defined exceptions with conditions, not blanket permission to broadcast. For Foundation, remember that international-disaster messages may be passed internationally on behalf of unlicensed people, and that User Service use has special provisions. Do not turn a casual exercise into an invented emergency exemption. [S1, S2]

### Encoding is not automatically encryption

Morse represents characters using a known code. Digital modes also represent information in particular ways. A listener needing the appropriate knowledge or decoder does not, by itself, make a transmission encrypted.

Normal amateur messages must not be encrypted to hide their meaning. The Foundation syllabus identifies exceptions for a direct request by a User Service and for amateur-satellite control. Ordinary private chat does not become an exception because an app offers an encryption button. [S1, S2]

Be considerate of a mixed-age audience. Threats and grossly offensive, obscene or menacing messages are not acceptable. A call sign identifies responsibility; it is not a shield from the rules governing message content. [S3]

## 5. Taking a station somewhere else

A UK amateur licence authorises operation in and over the UK, the Channel Islands and the Isle of Man, including their territorial seas, subject to its conditions. It also covers suitably registered ships or aircraft in international waters or airspace: the registration must be in the UK, Channel Islands or Isle of Man. [S2]

It does **not** itself authorise operation in another country's territory, territorial waters or airspace. For example, taking a Foundation handheld to France does not carry UK transmitting permission with it. Do not assume that the international arrangements available to Full licensees apply to Foundation.

For a ship or aircraft, permission from the captain or person currently in control is also necessary. That permission does not override frequency, power or geographical restrictions. Airborne operation is limited to permitted primary allocations and the stipulated **500 mW EIRP** limit; it is not the normal ground-operation power allowance. [S1, S2]

This is recognition-level teaching, not an instruction to transmit from an aircraft. Check the applicable conditions and operator's permission before planning any such activity.

## 6. Read the whole row, not just the power number

Use the current Foundation Licence Parameters in EX307 for exam practice. For a real station, use your actual licence conditions, applicable coordination requirements and current operating band plan.

Ask these questions in order:

1. Is the frequency within an allocation available to this licence?
2. Does the location or type of operation introduce a restriction?
3. What service status and sharing obligations apply?
4. What power limit applies, and how is that power defined?
5. Are the mode, occupied bandwidth and other operating conditions suitable?

The table contains several different power limits. "Foundation means 25 watts everywhere" is therefore a poor rule to learn.

| Worked lookup | What the referenced table tells you |
| --- | --- |
| 144-146 MHz, ordinary terrestrial use | Primary amateur allocation; 25 W maximum PEP |
| 2400-2450 MHz, ordinary terrestrial use | Secondary; 2 W maximum PEP; additional ISM-sharing condition |
| 431-432 MHz within 100 km of Charing Cross | Not available for amateur use under this allocation at that location |

**Roehampton example:** a proposed 431.500 MHz transmission falls inside that restricted range, and Roehampton is within the stated London radius. Reducing the power to 1 W does not remove a location exclusion. The lesson is a paper exercise, not a suggested transmission. [S2, S4]

**PEP**, **ERP** and **EIRP** are different ways of specifying power. Copy the unit and qualifier along with the number. Where ERP or EIRP is specified, the transmitter's displayed output alone is not sufficient because the antenna system matters. Antenna and power calculations return in later weeks.

Primary status does not necessarily mean exclusive use. Secondary services must protect primary services and cannot demand protection from their legitimate operation. All amateur operation must avoid undue interference.

## 7. Responsibility continues while you operate

A licensed operator is responsible for avoiding undue interference. If your activity appears to be causing a problem, pause the activity and investigate; do not simply increase power to overcome the complaint.

A person authorised by Ofcom can inspect the licence and equipment and may require modification, restriction or closedown. They may also require a log of specified details for a specified period and in a specified form. Keeping a personal hobby log is useful, but is different from such a requirement. [S1, S2]

EMF exposure, electrical safety and safe antenna installation still matter even when a frequency is permitted. This week flags those duties; the detailed EMF learning point and practical safety checks are scheduled for Week 6. Do not treat this lesson alone as approval for a live demonstration.

## 8. Put the rules to work

Before a contact, explain **who is operating, under whose licence, from where, on which frequency, with what settings, and for what purpose**. If any answer is uncertain, resolve it before transmitting.

Complete the worksheet and quiz. For every corrected answer, write the reasoning rather than memorising a letter. Bring one question about an unfamiliar licence-table row to the next session.
